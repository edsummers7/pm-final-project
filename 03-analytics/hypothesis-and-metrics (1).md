## Read your data snapshots
- **Does the funnel data confirm your M2 friction point, or does it tell a different story? Note where the numbers align with the qualitative pain you found and where they diverge.:** Where the data aligns with my M2 friction point

Findability is getting worse. Content searched then played fell from 41% to 34% (down 7 pts), so fewer searches end in someone watching. This matches the qualitative pain that search returns and discovery fail users in a large library.
The funnel leaks at the discovery stages. Of the visitors who browse (71%), about a third never open a title page (48%). Of those who reach a title page, about 40% never press play (29%). That fits the "overwhelmed by choice, can't find the one thing" pain.
Engagement is falling overall. Daily Active Users are down 21%, and average session length is down 23% (31 to 24 minutes). Users who can't find something they want are less likely to stay or return.

Where it diverges or adds nuance

The top of the funnel is holding. People are getting in and browsing, so the problem isn't acquisition. My M2 friction was framed as a search and navigation problem, but the largest leak is later in the funnel.
The biggest drop is after play starts. Only 38% of people who start playing reach a 30+ minute session (29% down to 11%), and sessions reaching 30+ minutes fell from 19% to 11%. Findability explains why people fail to start something, but not why those who do start don't stay. Relevance or content quality is probably a factor too.
Spotlight reach is only 18%, so most users haven't experienced any curation yet.

Conclusion: The data confirms the friction point but doesn't change my target. The cluttered library is still a real driver, because failed discovery shows up in search-to-play and in the browse-to-play steps. But I should reframe the problem around depth of engagement, which means helping users find something worth watching, not just something findable.
- **Do the retention patterns align with the workaround your M2 persona used to find content? Note what the Mo. 0→1 drop suggests about the onboarding experience your persona described as frustrating.:** Yes, they align strongly.

The Month 0 to Month 1 drop is the biggest leak in every cohort. Full Library cohorts lose 32 to 37 points in the first month (Sep 68%, Oct 66%, Nov 63%), and each month is slightly worse than the last.
That fits my persona's pain. Someone who can't find what they want in a cluttered library goes to Google or a competitor to find it and never comes back. The loss happens in the first weeks, before they've built a habit.
Spotlight cohorts lose less in Month 1 (26, 23 and 21 points), and they improve with each cohort (74%, 77%, 79%). So curation helps the onboarding experience.
It doesn't fully solve it. Even Spotlight cohorts lose a fifth or more of their users in Month 1, so first-session discovery is still the main leak to fix.
The Spotlight cohorts also hold up better later. The December cohort is at 52% in Month 3, against 34 to 39% for the Full Library cohorts.
- **Does the LTV gap and the content mix (61% trending for Wanderers) confirm the moment of misery your persona described? Note which segment your persona is in and whether the data confirms their pain.:** Wanderers are 41% of the base, the largest segment. They have the lowest LTV ($8.40), the fewest sessions (1.1 per week) and the most trending-heavy mix (61% trending, 18% curated).
That matches my persona's pain. They are overwhelmed by choice, default to whatever is surfaced as popular, and never reach the niche content that would deepen their engagement. Their low sessions per week suggest they have no reason to return.
Spotlight cuts Wanderer churn by 22 points, the biggest improvement of any segment. Casual Browsers improve by 14 points, and Power Users already have low churn.
This isn't a pivot. The segment with the weakest performance is also the one with the most to gain, so it is the high-leverage target. The business case rests on volume, not on LTV per user: moving a large share of the base up toward Casual Browser levels ($11.40 LTV, 2.3 sessions per week) matters more than a small gain from Power Users.
